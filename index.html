At first I went into meetings trying to keep up. Slowly that changed. I started asking more questions, reaching out for context, showing work before it was finished, and treating critique as information rather than a verdict.

CHAPTER 03

Objectives Were Hard to Keep Alive

Once I got into OYS Objective Tracker and the research behind it, the real problem moved. Setting an objective was the easy part. The work that proved it happened across meetings, emails, chats and documents, and months later people had to remember all of it and connect it back.

The challenge wasn't setting the objective. It was keeping it alive.

CHAPTER 04

From Tracking Goals to Owning Stories

So I stopped treating an objective as a record to maintain. Targets, signals, insights, reflection and actions were not fields on a form. They were the story forming around the work.

An objective isn't just something you set. It's a story that develops through your work.

CHAPTER 05

The Accordion Moment

I inherited an existing table-based experience, with objectives sorted into fixed columns. As targets, signals, insights and reflection came together, the story started outgrowing the row. I explored four structures: an accordion, a master-detail split, a card grid and a drawer.

The accordion held up best. It stays scannable collapsed and gives the objective room when opened. Feedback later showed the hierarchy and labels still needed work, which was fair.

The table organized the information. The accordion gave the story room to breathe.

CHAPTER 06

Objectives Should Not Be Maintained Alone

If the evidence already exists across everyday work, why should anyone rebuild it from memory at review time? Copilot can surface possible work signals and connect them to the objective they support, with the source visible.

The open question is fatigue. Ask for approval on everything and the help becomes homework. Remove approval and the person loses ownership of their own story. I don't think that tension is solved yet.

OYS · TRUST PRINCIPLE: Copilot finds. You decide.

CHAPTER 07

Exploring Autonomous Agents

AI incubation stretched the question from what a screen should do to what a system should be allowed to do. Exploring it alongside Zina Ndugwa and Harrison Maina, I looked at agents carrying context across tools, and what that demands: permission, consent, reversible actions, and autonomy scaled to consequence.

This stayed exploration. Nothing shipped, and the hardest questions are still open.

The more AI can do, the more carefully we have to decide what it should do.

CHAPTER 08

Building Bigger Together

PortPilot was something I built alongside my fellow interns, bringing our different skills together around one ambitious idea: an agent-driven workflow for making application porting to Windows on Arm easier to analyze, test, validate, and reuse.

Beyond the technology, what stayed with me was the experience of building together, sharing ideas, solving problems, and turning something complex into a story we could collectively explain.

PortPilot taught me that ambitious ideas become stronger when you build them together.

CHAPTER 09

Sentry: Designing for Trust

What if the scam comes from a number nobody has reported yet? Reputation lists only catch what has already happened to someone else. Sentry looked at the pattern instead: the message, the link, and above all what is actually being said during the call.

It never hangs up for you. Models get things wrong often enough that automatic blocking would cost people real calls.

SENTRY · SAFETY PRINCIPLE: AI can warn and explain. The final decision stays with you.

CHAPTER 10

Fursa: Designing for Second Chances

Fursa was built with Clean Start Africa, connecting formerly incarcerated women to support, skills, local chapters and opportunities. Volunteering alongside them changed how I approached it.

They already understood the problem better than any of us. We were not there to replace that with technology. We were there to help a mission they had already built travel further, from concept into a working prototype.

Sometimes design isn't about inventing a new mission. It's about helping an existing one go further.

CHAPTER 11

What the Setbacks Actually Taught Me

Not everything worked. Directions needed revision. Feedback exposed hierarchy I thought was obvious. Prototypes surfaced problems static screens had hidden. Signal approval raised a fatigue question I could not close, and the agent work stayed exploratory.

  • OYS Objective Tracker: Understand the problem before you change the interface.
  • Autonomous agents: More autonomy means more responsibility, not less design.
  • PortPilot: A complex idea is only real once a team can explain its value.
  • Sentry: Trust is designed. It comes from control, not from confidence.
  • Fursa: Listen to the people already closest to the problem.
I stopped seeing every setback as something to hide. Usually it was the moment that showed me what needed to change next.

CHAPTER 13

From Static Screens to Something You Could Click

Static frames let people react to layout. Rebuilt as a working prototype in Copilot, the flow could be walked instead of described, and the feedback changed with it. People stopped reacting to the screen and started reacting to the sequence, the waiting, the approval, and the moment the reflection finally appears.

A static frame showed the interface. The prototype revealed the experience.
IT WAS NEVER JUST ABOUT THE WORK

The people around the projects mattered too.

When I think about my internship, I don't only remember objectives, prototypes and design reviews. I remember the people.

A lunch conversation would turn into career advice. A coffee chat would become a lesson about design. Some of my favourite moments happened nowhere near a design file.

LUNCH, AND THE ADVICE THAT CAME WITH IT

The lessons that were never on a syllabus

I got to learn from designers like Tim Chege and Eric Ochieng: asking the questions I was still nervous to ask, and hearing the kind of lessons that never show up in a design course. Even a virtual conversation with Shweta Ratanpura left advice that stayed with me long after the meeting ended.

Not much of it was about becoming a better designer. It was about confidence, curiosity, navigating uncertainty without pretending it isn't there, and finding my own path instead of copying someone else's.

They weren't handing me answers. They were showing me how they had learned to ask better questions.
THE GARAGE AND HACKATHON SEASON

I signed up to help run an event. I found a community.

Volunteering at the Microsoft Global Hackathon with Lydiah Karanja, GD Dhanjal and the rest of the crew meant registrations, planning, Garage activities, helping participants find projects to join, and solving small problems all day long.

It showed me another side of Microsoft: one where experimentation is encouraged, and where people give their time simply because they enjoy helping others build things.

Being part of that community made a very large company feel much smaller, and much more human.
THE INTERN COMMUNITY

A group of strangers, slowly becoming friends

Somewhere between breakfasts, speaker sessions, Garage days and random chats around the office, a group of strangers slowly became friends.

We were all figuring things out at the same time, celebrating each other's wins, laughing at our mistakes, and trying to make the most of a few short months.

Nobody had it all figured out yet. That is exactly what made it so easy to belong.
THE INTERN HACK DANCE

Yes, we actually did this

Song suggestions. A long search for something Kenyan enough. The slow discovery that the moves look considerably easier on YouTube than they do in an office corridor.

For a few minutes nobody was an intern buried in a Hackathon project. We just showed up, danced, laughed and enjoyed being a team.

We tried. We danced. Perfection left the building pretty early.
THE GIVEAWAY, AND ONE SMALL TROPHY

Solitaire Champion

The intern giveaway near the end felt less like an event and more like a reunion. Games, friendly competition, jokes, photos, and the bittersweet realization that this was nearly over.

I won the Solitaire challenge. Completely unrelated to product design, and somehow one of the moments I remember most. The trophy didn't matter. The people I was sharing it with did.

I came in to learn how to design. I left with a story about the people I got to learn beside.

The work taught me how Microsoft builds products. The people taught me what makes it worth building there.
